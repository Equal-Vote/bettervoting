# Electing More Than One Winner

When a race has more than one seat, BetterVoting asks you a second question: **Basic** or **Proportional**. It is the most consequential choice in the product, and the one most often made by accident.

**The one-sentence version:** Basic elects the strongest winners; Proportional elects a representative mix. They routinely produce completely different sets of winners from identical ballots.

## What each one does

**Basic Multi-Winner** elects the first winner exactly as a single-winner race would, then repeats the whole process for the next seat, and again, until every seat is full.

**Proportional Multi-Winner** works out a quota — roughly, how many supporters a candidate needs to earn one seat — and fills seats so that any group large enough to meet that quota wins one.

## Why the difference is not subtle

Imagine a club of 100 members electing 3 board seats. 60 members share one outlook; 40 share another.

**Under Basic**, the 60-member group is the majority in *every single seat election*. They can win the first seat, then the second, then the third. **A 60% group can take 100% of the seats**, and nothing has gone wrong — that's the method working as designed.

**Under Proportional**, the 40-member group is comfortably more than one third of the electorate, so they meet the quota for a seat. The likely outcome is 2 seats to the larger group and 1 to the smaller.

Same ballots. Same voters. Different boards.

## Which should you pick?

Ask what the seats are *for*.

**Basic** fits when the seats are interchangeable and you want the best occupants:

* a hiring committee choosing three finalists to interview
* picking the five best submissions to a competition
* electing officers where each seat should go to whoever has the broadest support
* any case where you'd be pleased if one faction's candidates were simply the strongest

**Proportional** fits when the body is meant to *represent* the people voting for it:

* a board or council representing a membership
* a steering committee meant to reflect the different groups in an organisation
* any case where a group being persistently outvoted and getting no seats at all would be a failure

The failure mode worth naming: **if you're filling seats meant to represent people and you pick Basic, you may be surprised by the result** — and the surprise arrives after the election, when it can't be changed.

## Which methods support which

| | Basic | Proportional |
|:---|:---|:---|
| STAR Voting | ✓ | via **Proportional STAR** |
| Approval Voting | ✓ | |
| Ranked Robin | ✓ | |
| Ranked Choice Voting | ✓ | via **Single Transferable Vote** |
| Choose One | ✓ | |

Proportional representation has its own two methods rather than being a switch on the others: **Proportional STAR** if you want a scored ballot, **STV** if you want a ranked one. The ballot your voters fill in is the same as the single-winner version — only the counting changes.

## A note on explaining it

Proportional methods are harder to explain to voters than their single-winner counterparts, and that's the real cost of choosing one. It's worth writing a sentence or two in your election description saying what you're doing and why — something like *"three seats, counted proportionally, so that a group of roughly a third of members can expect a seat."*

Voters rarely object to proportionality. They object to being surprised by a result they can't account for.
